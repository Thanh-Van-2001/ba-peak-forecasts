Do Grid Operators Under-Forecast Peak Load? Selection, Heat and a Decade of Day-Ahead Forecasts in U.S. Balancing Authorities

A public scorecard reports that the day-ahead load forecasts of U.S. balancing authorities (BAs) are below demand at the daily peak hour on about 60% of days. Evaluating forecasts at times chosen by the outcome makes even calibrated forecasts look low (the forecaster's dilemma). To our knowledge, no published evaluation of operators' forecasts separates peak bias from this selection. Using a pre-specified plan and EIA-930 data for 43 BAs (164 thousand BA-days, 2016–2026), we compare the median daily-peak error on the 5% of days with the highest actual peak and the 5% with the highest forecast peak. On actual-peak days the forecast was low in 32 of 43 BAs (Holm p = 0.004; not significant by region); on forecast-peak days in only 18 (p = 0.89). Judged by daily maxima, not at the peak hour, it was low on 54% of BA-days. We find no evidence of a systematic under-forecast of peak days; exploratory bounds exclude a median forecast-peak bias below −0.56%, although some BAs were low. The scorecard figure is consistent with selection. On days whose observed temperature exceeded the summer 95th percentile, the forecast was lower than on other summer days in 30 of 43 BAs (Holm p = 0.02; pooled −0.56 percentage points; not significant by region), mostly on a hot spell's first day. This is error given realised heat, an outcome, not a bias visible a day ahead. Summer-day accuracy did not change between 2016–2019 and 2023–2026. Scorecards should condition on the forecast.

Keywords: Load forecasting, peak demand, forecast evaluation, heat waves, EIA-930.
