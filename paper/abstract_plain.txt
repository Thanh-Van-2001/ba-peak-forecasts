Do Grid Operators Under-Forecast Peak Load? Selection, Heat and a Decade of Day-Ahead Forecasts in U.S. Balancing Authorities

A public scorecard reports that the day-ahead load forecasts of U.S. balancing authorities (BAs) are below demand at the daily peak hour on about 60% of days. Evaluating forecasts at times chosen by the outcome makes even calibrated forecasts look low (the forecaster's dilemma). Published evaluations of operators' forecasts do not, to our knowledge, separate peak bias from this selection. Using a pre-specified plan and EIA-930 data for 43 BAs (164 thousand BA-days, 2016–2026), we compare the median error of the daily peak on the 5% of days with the highest actual peak and on the 5% with the highest forecast peak. On actual-peak days the forecast was low in 32 of 43 BAs (Holm p = 0.004); on forecast-peak days in only 18 (p = 0.89). By the daily maximum rather than at the peak hour, the forecast was low on 54% of BA-days. In exploratory analyses, forecasts on forecast-peak days exceeded each BA's usual bias in 35 of 43 BAs. We find no evidence of a systematic under-forecast of peak days; the scorecard figure reflects selection. On days above the 95th percentile of summer temperature, the forecast was lower than on other summer days in 30 of 43 BAs (Holm p = 0.02; pooled −0.56 percentage points), a shortfall that exploratory analyses place on the first day of a hot spell. Summer-day accuracy did not change between 2016–2019 and 2023–2026. Scorecards should condition on the forecast, and operators should watch the onset of heat.

Keywords: Load forecasting, peak demand, forecast evaluation, heat waves, EIA-930.
